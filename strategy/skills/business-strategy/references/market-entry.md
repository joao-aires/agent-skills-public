# Market Entry

## Purpose

Market entry decides where to concentrate first, through which route, and what would make the entry succeed or stop. It is narrower than overall strategy: the destination may be larger than the first market.

Use it when a company, product line or geography is being added, or when an existing position must move into an adjacent market. Use [wedge selection](wedge-selection.md) for the entry-point logic itself and [market system mapping](market-system-mapping.md) for the actors and money flows around it.

## Frame the entry decision

State before analysing:

```text
Decision: enter / not enter / enter later, and through which mode
Target: segment, job, geography, layer
Objective: revenue, learning, distribution, capability or defence
Horizon: time to first evidence and time to durable position
Constraints: capital, people, regulation, brand, channel, platform dependencies
Options: including do nothing and a delayed or partnered entry
What would change the decision:
```

A vague "should we expand to Europe?" becomes "should we enter the German mid-market with a partner-led motion in the next four quarters, and what evidence would stop the expansion after the first two?"

## Select the beachhead

A beachhead is a coherent group of customers that one product and one go-to-market motion can serve better than anyone else, and from which expansion is easier.

Evaluate candidates on:

- **Shared circumstance** — same trigger, workflow, buying criteria and language.
- **Pain and urgency** — a cost the customer already recognizes and acts on.
- **Willingness to pay** — a budget owner with discretion, not only enthusiasm.
- **Reachability** — a concentrated channel, community, association or partner.
- **Dominance potential** — a plausible path to being the default or strongly preferred choice, not a thin presence.
- **Accumulation** — what winning it creates: references, data, integrations, standards influence, delivery learning.
- **Expansion geometry** — which next market becomes easier because this one was won.
- **Entry barriers** — certification, local presence, language, procurement norms, regulation, data residency.

Prefer a market that can be won deeply over a large market entered thinly. But reject a manufactured niche: "no competitors" can mean an overlooked opportunity or no demand.

Ask:

> If we win here, what do we own that we did not own before, and which next move does it unlock?

If the answer is only revenue, the beachhead is a sales territory, not a strategic entry.

## Judge market attractiveness

Use the sizing and structural references rather than a single market number.

- **Size and obtainability** — bottom-up addressable spend, reachable accounts and realistic penetration; see [market sizing](market-sizing.md).
- **Structure and profit pool** — who holds bargaining power, where margin accumulates, which layer controls access; see [market system mapping](market-system-mapping.md).
- **Competitive intensity** — number and strength of alternatives, bundling risk, internal build and open-source substitutes; see [competitive dynamics](competitive-dynamics.md).
- **Growth and timing** — whether the market is expanding, consolidating or shifting budget, and what structural change creates the opening.
- **Uncertainty** — which future states would make the entry attractive or irrelevant; see [future market scenarios](future-market-scenarios.md).
- **Access** — whether the required channel, partner or certification is available on acceptable terms.
- **Fit** — whether the entry strengthens the existing advantage or dilutes it.

Separate current attractiveness from future attractiveness. An entry that is small today can be correct if the budget category is moving toward it; an entry that is large today can be wrong if the profit pool is migrating away.

Do not average these dimensions into one score. A single fatal constraint — no budget owner, no channel, a regulatory bar, a platform that can bundle the capability — overrides a strong total.

## Choose an entry mode

Compare modes on control, speed, cost, learning and reversibility.

| Mode | Use when | Main gain | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct entry | The offer fits with modest adaptation and the company can reach buyers | Control, learning, margin | Cost and slow ramp |
| Partner or reseller | Local reach, certification or language is the binding constraint | Speed and access | Dependency, diluted learning, channel conflict |
| Acquisition | Capability, customers or regulatory position is scarce and time matters | Instant position and talent | Integration, culture, hidden liabilities |
| Licensing or OEM | Another product owns the workflow or distribution | Reach without full build-out | Commoditization, weak customer relationship |
| Marketplace or embedded | The buyer already works inside a platform ecosystem | Distribution and trust transfer | Platform terms, visibility, data limits |
| Community or open-source-led | Developers or technical buyers self-select and share knowledge | Adoption, credibility, feedback | Monetization separation, support burden |
| Services-first | The problem requires configuration or trust before productization | Learning and cash | Non-reusable work, unclear product boundary |

Entry-mode questions:

- Which mode produces the learning the strategy needs, not only the fastest revenue?
- What does the mode foreclose — direct relationships, pricing freedom, future channel options?
- Can the mode be reversed without losing the customer or the capability?
- Who owns the customer relationship and the data?
- Does the partner's incentive align with the beachhead's success or with its own product agenda?

A partner that can sell but cannot support the product often produces early revenue and later churn.

## Sequence the entry

Sequence by evidence gates, not by calendar enthusiasm.

```text
reference segment
→ repeatable delivery and proof
→ adjacent segment or geography
→ broader motion
→ durable position
```

For each step define:

- what must be true to move on;
- which asset or credibility the previous step created;
- the cost and reversibility of the next commitment;
- the signal that would stop the sequence.

Common sequences:

- **Segment depth first** — same geography, deeper workflow, more roles.
- **Workflow adjacency** — same customer, next job in the chain.
- **Geography replication** — same segment, new market with adapted channel and compliance.
- **Layer move** — from application to platform, or from service to product.

A sequence that requires a different customer, channel and product at every step is not one strategy; it is several bets sharing a balance sheet.

## Competitor profile

Profile only competitors that can change the entry decision. A feature matrix is not a profile.

For each:

```text
Competitor:
Segment and job they serve:
Why customers choose them today:
What they do materially better than we would:
Where their model constrains them (not "they are weak"):
  - customer they must protect
  - channel or partner they cannot bypass
  - pricing or cost structure they cannot match
  - product or architecture they cannot change quickly
  - incentive that makes this market unattractive to them
How they are likely to respond to our entry:
What would make them respond faster or harder:
Evidence and date:
```

The two decisive questions:

1. **What do they do better?** Name it plainly. An entry plan that assumes the incumbent is bad at the job is usually wrong about the customer.
2. **Where do they leave the market open?** An opening is structural: a segment they cannot serve profitably, a job their product is mispriced for, a channel they cannot use without cannibalizing, a service level they will not provide, a geography they deprioritize.

A gap is not an opening merely because the competitor has not shipped something. It may be unprofitable, technically constrained, legally barred or strategically irrelevant to them. Distinguish:

- **unserved** — no one serves it;
- **underserved** — served badly at the current price or service level;
- **structurally abandoned** — incumbents cannot serve it without damaging their position;
- **temporarily ignored** — available to anyone who moves, including the incumbent once it notices.

**Structurally abandoned** is a durable opening. **Unserved** and **underserved** markets are openings only when the incumbent's inability to respond is structural. **Temporarily ignored** is a race, not an opening: anyone who moves can take it, including the incumbent once it notices.

Also include non-obvious competition: internal build, spreadsheets, consultants, adjacent platforms bundling a "good enough" version, and open source. See [competitive dynamics](competitive-dynamics.md) for the three competition levels.

## Monitoring and decision triggers

Entry plans fail quietly when no one defines what would stop them. Define:

| Trigger | Signal | Threshold and window | Decision it reopens |
|---|---|---|---|
| Demand | Qualified pipeline from the beachhead | Fewer than 8 qualified accounts in two quarters | Continue / narrow / stop |
| Delivery | Time to first value and implementation hours | Median above 30 days or 40 hours for two quarters | Mode or product change |
| Economics | CAC payback | Above 18 months without improvement | Pricing, channel or segment change |
| Competition | Incumbent launch, price move, bundling | A bundled offer at no incremental cost to the buyer | Wedge or positioning change |
| Channel | Partner productivity and conflict | Under half of partner-sourced deals reaching evaluation | Mode change |
| Regulation | Certification or data residency requirement | A required approval unavailable within the entry window | Market or sequence change |

These thresholds are illustrative. Set them from the decision and the plan, not by copying this table.

Monitoring rules:

- Assign an owner and a review date to each trigger.
- Prefer leading indicators; revenue alone reports the outcome too late to change it cheaply.
- Distinguish a trigger that fires from a decision that changes. A fired trigger with a reaffirmed thesis is still evidence; record why.
- Review the entry thesis when a competitor changes the basis of competition, not only when the numbers miss.

## Output

End with:

- the recommended beachhead and why it is coherent;
- the entry mode and its trade-offs;
- the sequence with evidence gates;
- the competitor profile, including what they do better and where the opening is structural;
- estimated economics tied to [market sizing](market-sizing.md) and [pricing and packaging](pricing-and-packaging.md);
- the main risks and the future states that would invalidate the entry;
- monitoring triggers, owners and review dates;
- the cost of being wrong and the exit or pause path.

The useful result is a defensible first market and a testable route into it, not a country-by-country market list.
