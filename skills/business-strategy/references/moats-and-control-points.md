# Moats and Strategic Control Points

## Principle

Creating customer value and capturing durable economic value are different problems.

Use `economic-power-and-value-capture.md` first when the question is **whether the business can retain value**. Use this reference when the question is **what makes that advantage persist and compound**.

A product can be useful, fast-growing, and still be strategically weak if customers can switch easily or a platform can absorb the capability.

## Conventional defensibility

Evaluate relevant sources of durability such as:

- switching costs / workflow lock-in
- proprietary data or accumulated context
- network effects
- ecosystem / marketplace
- distribution advantage
- brand / trust
- standards adoption
- scale economies
- embedded integrations
- regulatory / compliance advantage
- operational complexity customers do not want to own
- unique supply or scarce resources

Do not claim a moat merely because one of these nouns appears. Explain the causal mechanism and how it affects future bargaining power, customer choice, or cost structure.

## Accumulating asset

Ask:

> What gets stronger as more customers use the product or as the company exists for longer?

Possible assets:

- data
- workflow history
- integrations
- community
- developer mindshare
- partner ecosystem
- reputation
- operational learning
- installed base
- standards influence

If nothing accumulates, the company may need exceptional distribution or product velocity to remain differentiated.

## Control-point test

Ask:

> If this company became the default layer for the workflow, what valuable downstream activity would pass through it?

Potential control surfaces include:

- identity / authorization
- workflow state
- transaction flow
- distribution
- data access
- policy / governance
- marketplace discovery
- integration routing
- system of record

A control point is valuable when other activity depends on it and bypassing it is meaningfully costly **because the layer creates value**, not merely because it creates friction.

## Control versus bottleneck

A temporary bottleneck is not automatically a durable control point.

Test:

- Can standards remove the bottleneck?
- Can customers multi-home?
- Can an incumbent bundle around it?
- Does the control point create value or merely friction?
- Is bypassing technically easy?
- Does regulation force interoperability?

## Commoditization risk

For every proposed moat ask what happens if the underlying technology becomes 10× cheaper or easier.

Strong strategies often move toward complements that become more valuable as the core technology commoditizes.

Separate:

- what is proprietary today;
- what is likely to become standard;
- what advantage survives after that happens.

## Platform dependency

Assess dependency on:

- model providers
- cloud platforms
- app stores
- social networks
- payment rails
- operating systems
- data providers

A company can have a good product moat but poor bargaining power against a critical platform.

## Open-source dynamics

Open source can create developer mindshare, ecosystem, standards influence, and distribution while simultaneously commoditizing the software itself.

The moat may live in:

- managed operations
- enterprise trust
- proprietary control plane
- ecosystem
- data
- distribution
- integration depth

Evaluate adoption advantage separately from monetization defensibility.

## Last-mover durability test

Project the market forward to a mature state.

Ask:

- Which current advantages disappear once technology improves?
- Which assets compound with installed base, time, or ecosystem participation?
- Which parts of the stack are likely to be bundled?
- What can customers switch, multi-home, or build internally by then?
- Why does this company still hold an unusual position?

The strongest answer is usually an accumulating mechanism, not "we keep innovating faster forever."

## Durability test

For each claimed moat state:

```text
Asset / mechanism:
Why customers care:
Why it strengthens with scale/time:
How it creates economic power:
How competitors attack it:
How customers bypass it:
What future change weakens it:
Confidence:
```

## Strategic implication

The objective is not "have the most moats."

Choose a wedge and expansion sequence that progressively improves customer value, bargaining power, retention, distribution, and control over something customers genuinely value.

Then ask whether that position remains unusually strong after the market matures.
