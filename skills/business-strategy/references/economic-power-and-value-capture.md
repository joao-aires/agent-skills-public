# Economic Power and Value Capture

## Principle

Creating exceptional customer value and building an exceptional business are related but different problems.

A useful reasoning model is:

```text
business quality ≈ value creation × value capture × durability
```

This is not a financial formula. It is a forcing function: a strategy is weak if any of the three terms is structurally weak.

Ask two separate questions:

1. **Value creation** — how much better is the customer's outcome?
2. **Value capture** — why can the company retain a meaningful share of that value instead of having it competed, bargained, or passed through to others?

A large market or useful product does not imply attractive economics. Conversely, apparent pricing power without genuine value creation may be fragile, extractive, or vulnerable to regulation and entry.

## Economic-power chain

Trace the causal mechanism rather than labeling a company a "monopoly" or saying it has a moat.

```text
customer value created
→ magnitude / scarcity of differentiation
→ availability of substitutes
→ customer bargaining power
→ supplier / platform bargaining power
→ ease of entry and imitation
→ switching / search / integration costs
→ pricing discretion or structural cost advantage
→ value captured
```

The objective is not literal absence of competitors. It is a position in which direct competition does not continuously erase the company's economic surplus.

## Sources of economic power

Evaluate only mechanisms that are causally relevant.

### Differentiated value

The product produces an outcome that alternatives cannot match on an important buying criterion.

Test the **magnitude**, not merely the existence, of differentiation.

Possible conclusions:

- incremental
- meaningfully better
- dramatically better
- qualitatively new / previously impossible

Use a "10×" idea as a heuristic, not a literal threshold. The important question is:

> Is the advantage large enough to change purchasing behavior or the customer's comparison set?

### Structural cost advantage

The company can sustainably deliver equivalent value at materially lower cost because of scale, process, proprietary supply, technology, distribution, or another durable mechanism.

Do not confuse temporary subsidy or lower margin with cost advantage.

### Network / ecosystem effects

The product becomes more valuable or harder to replace as participation grows.

State whether the effect is local, cross-side, data-based, marketplace-based, community-based, or another concrete mechanism.

### Switching, integration, and workflow costs

Customers accumulate state, integrations, habits, approvals, data, or organizational dependencies that make replacement meaningfully costly.

Distinguish value-creating embeddedness from artificial lock-in.

### Distribution power

The company owns scarce or compounding access to customers: community, channel, default placement, partnerships, brand, installed base, or another repeatable advantage.

### Standards / control points

Important activity passes through a layer the company controls: identity, policy, transaction flow, workflow state, distribution, system of record, data access, integration routing, or a standard/ecosystem interface.

Use `moats-and-control-points.md` for the detailed durability test.

### Brand / trust / regulatory position

Reputation, certification, regulatory approval, safety record, or trusted status can create real choice asymmetry when customers cannot cheaply reproduce the trust themselves.

## Bargaining-power analysis

For every attractive opportunity ask where created value leaks.

### Customer power

- Are buyers concentrated?
- Can they credibly switch or multi-home?
- Is procurement standardized around price?
- Can they build internally?
- Does the product own a budget or merely consume another vendor's budget?

### Supplier / platform power

- Are critical inputs controlled by a small number of vendors?
- Can a cloud/model/app-store/platform provider raise prices or restrict access?
- Can the platform bundle the product's differentiation?
- Does the company create demand that another layer monetizes more effectively?

### Entry and imitation

- How expensive is replication?
- How long does replication take?
- What must a follower reproduce besides features: distribution, data, trust, ecosystem, workflow history, regulation, scale?
- Does the market reward fast followers almost as much as the pioneer?

### Substitution

- What different product or behavior solves the same job?
- Does a new standard or cheaper underlying technology remove the problem entirely?

## Pricing-power test

Do not infer pricing power from high current prices.

Test whether the company can:

- charge materially above marginal cost without rapid substitution;
- raise price while preserving attractive retention;
- price against value rather than implementation cost;
- avoid procurement collapsing the product into a commodity comparison;
- preserve gross margin as suppliers and competitors react.

Also recognize **non-price economic power**: a structural cost advantage can create superior returns even without premium pricing.

## Beachhead dominance

A narrow market can be strategically superior to a large horizontal market when it is coherent enough to win deeply.

Ask:

- Is the beachhead a real customer group with common needs, buying behavior, and distribution?
- Can the company plausibly become a default or strongly preferred choice?
- Does high penetration create learning, reputation, network effects, data, integrations, or another advantage?
- Does winning this beachhead make the next market easier?

Do not manufacture a tiny category merely to claim high share. "No competitors" can mean either an unexplored opportunity or no meaningful demand.

Use `wedge-selection.md` for the entry and sequencing mechanics.

## Expansion and power

Growth is not automatically strategically accretive.

For each adjacency ask:

> Does entering this market increase or dilute our economic power?

Expansion may strengthen power by increasing:

- network density
- data advantage
- distribution
- installed base
- ecosystem participation
- switching costs
- standards influence
- bargaining leverage

It may weaken power by moving into generic segments, requiring incompatible products, increasing buyer power, or creating direct conflict with stronger incumbents.

## Last-mover / mature-market test

Assume the company succeeds and the market matures over roughly 7–10 years.

Ask:

- What has commoditized?
- What has standardized?
- What can customers now build or multi-home?
- What did platforms bundle?
- Who consolidated?
- What became dramatically cheaper?
- What advantage accumulated inside the company?
- Who has bargaining power now?
- Why can the company still earn attractive returns?

A good long-term thesis explains **what compounds as the rest of the stack becomes easier**.
