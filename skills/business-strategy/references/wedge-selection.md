# Wedge Selection

## Purpose

The wedge is the first product, customer, workflow, or distribution entry point that earns adoption and creates a path toward the larger strategy.

A good wedge is not simply the smallest product. It should make the next strategic move easier.

## Separate wedge from destination

For each candidate define:

- **Wedge** — what is adopted first
- **Immediate value** — why the customer adopts now
- **Accumulating asset** — what becomes more valuable with use
- **Expansion surface** — what adjacent use cases / teams / products follow
- **Control point** — what durable strategic position the wedge can lead toward

```text
wedge
→ adoption / distribution
→ learning + accumulated assets
→ expansion
→ stronger economics / switching costs
→ strategic position
```

If the arrows are weak, the wedge may be a good product but a poor entry strategy.

## Candidate generation

Do not compare only variants of the original idea.

Generate alternatives across:

- customer segment
- user versus buyer
- use case
- geography
- channel
- product layer
- workflow location
- open-source boundary
- pricing / monetization
- high-touch versus self-serve

## Evaluation dimensions

Score qualitatively or 1–5 when useful.

### Customer pull

- pain intensity
- urgency
- frequency
- clear trigger
- willingness to change

### Adoption

- time-to-value
- implementation burden
- behavior change
- trust requirements
- integration requirements

### Commercial clarity

- identifiable buyer
- existing budget
- plausible pricing metric
- reachable accounts
- sales-cycle fit

### Distribution

- low-cost channel access
- community / ecosystem leverage
- viral or collaborative exposure
- partnerships
- founder advantage

### Competition

- direct density
- bundled alternatives
- internal build
- incumbent reaction
- ease of copying

### Strategic leverage

- data / workflow / integration accumulation
- ecosystem growth
- switching costs
- expansion surface
- cross-sell
- movement toward a control point

### Economics

- gross margin
- support burden
- CAC potential
- ACV / usage expansion
- capital intensity

### Future resilience

- dependency on today's technology architecture
- dependency on a temporary market transition
- value across plausible future scenarios

## Use scoring correctly

A scorecard is for exposing trade-offs.

Do not let a weighted average hide fatal flaws. A wedge with a high total score but no buyer, no distribution path, or extreme platform risk may still be unacceptable.

Mark **gating conditions** separately.

Example:

```text
Wedge A score: 4.2 / 5
Gating issue: requires regulator approval that is unlikely within 24 months.
Verdict: not first wedge despite high theoretical attractiveness.
```

## Sequencing

Sometimes two wedges are both useful but in different order.

Ask:

- Which wedge creates credibility for the next?
- Which generates proprietary learning?
- Which creates integrations or installed base?
- Which can fund expansion?
- Does an early wedge trap positioning in a low-value category?

## Wedge recommendation

End with:

1. recommended wedge
2. why now
3. customer and buyer
4. time-to-value
5. distribution path
6. what it accumulates
7. next expansion step
8. biggest risk
9. evidence that would cause selection of a different wedge
