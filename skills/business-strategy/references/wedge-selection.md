# Wedge Selection

## Purpose

The wedge is the first product, customer, workflow, geography, or distribution entry point that earns adoption and creates a path toward the larger strategy.

A good wedge is not simply the smallest product or the largest immediate market. It should be **coherent enough to win deeply** and make the next strategic move easier.

## Separate wedge from destination

For each candidate define:

- **Wedge** — what is adopted first
- **Immediate value** — why the customer adopts now
- **Beachhead** — the coherent market or workflow in which the wedge can become strongly preferred
- **Accumulating asset** — what becomes more valuable with use
- **Expansion surface** — what adjacent use cases / teams / products follow
- **Control point / power mechanism** — what durable strategic position the wedge can lead toward

```text
wedge
→ beachhead adoption / distribution
→ learning + accumulated assets
→ expansion
→ stronger economics / switching costs / bargaining power
→ strategic position
```

If the arrows are weak, the wedge may be a good product but a poor entry strategy.

## Candidate generation

Do not compare only variants of the original idea.

Generate alternatives across:

- customer segment
- user versus buyer
- use case / job
- geography
- channel
- product layer
- workflow location
- open-source boundary
- pricing / monetization
- high-touch versus self-serve

For `explore` and `develop` work, also generate at least one alternative from a **different underlying belief about the market**, not just a different segment or packaging choice.

## Contrarian / secret hypothesis

Use this when the opportunity is novel or the market consensus itself may be wrong.

Ask:

```text
What does the market broadly believe?
What evidence supports that belief?
What might the market be wrong about?
What do we believe instead?
Why might that belief be true?
If true, what business becomes possible?
Why has nobody exploited it yet?
```

A contrarian belief is not good merely because it is unpopular.

```text
non-consensus + wrong = bad strategy
non-consensus + right + economically meaningful = opportunity
```

Treat the belief as a high-impact assumption and define evidence that would falsify it.

## Beachhead coherence

A narrow entry market is attractive only if it is **real**, not artificially defined to make competition disappear.

Test whether customers share:

- an urgent or frequent problem
- similar workflow/context
- similar buying criteria
- similar willingness to pay
- reachable distribution channels
- enough social, professional, or ecosystem connection for reputation/learning to compound

Ask:

> Could one product and one GTM motion plausibly become a default or strongly preferred choice for this group?

"No competitors" is ambiguous. It can indicate an overlooked opportunity or insufficient demand.

## Dominance potential

Do not ask only:

> How large is this wedge?

Also ask:

> How completely can we win it, and what does winning it buy us?

Evaluate:

- plausible penetration and share of attention/workflow
- speed to meaningful adoption
- concentration of distribution
- incumbent response
- switching and multi-homing
- local network / community effects
- whether high penetration creates reputation, data, integrations, standards, or learning

Do not impose a universal market-share threshold. The strategic question is whether a concentrated position creates materially more leverage than a thin presence in a much larger market.

## Magnitude / non-comparability test

A wedge that is merely a little better may be forced into feature and price competition.

For the customer's important buying criteria, compare the best alternative with the proposed product:

| Dimension | Proposed product | Best alternative | Magnitude / consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Time |  |  |  |
| Cost |  |  |  |
| Quality / accuracy |  |  |  |
| Effort / friction |  |  |  |
| Risk |  |  |  |
| Capability |  |  |  |

Classify the overall advantage as:

- incremental
- meaningfully better
- dramatically better
- qualitatively new / previously impossible

A "10×" advantage is a heuristic, not a literal requirement. The key question is:

> Is the difference large enough to change behavior or the customer's comparison set?

## Evaluation dimensions

Score qualitatively or 1–5 when useful.

### Customer pull

- pain intensity
- urgency
- frequency
- clear trigger
- willingness to change

### Adoption

- time-to-value
- implementation burden
- behavior change
- trust requirements
- integration requirements

### Commercial clarity

- identifiable buyer
- existing budget
- plausible pricing metric
- reachable accounts
- sales-cycle fit

### Distribution

- low-cost channel access
- community / ecosystem leverage
- viral or collaborative exposure
- partnerships
- founder advantage

### Competition and power

- direct density
- bundled alternatives
- internal build
- incumbent reaction
- ease of copying
- beachhead coherence
- dominance potential
- magnitude of differentiation
- value-capture potential

### Strategic leverage

- data / workflow / integration accumulation
- ecosystem growth
- switching costs
- expansion surface
- cross-sell
- movement toward a control point

### Economics

- gross margin
- support burden
- CAC potential
- ACV / usage expansion
- capital intensity

### Future resilience

- dependency on today's technology architecture
- dependency on a temporary market transition
- value across plausible future scenarios
- whether expansion increases or dilutes economic power

## Expansion geometry

Do not describe expansion as a list of adjacent markets. Show the causal sequence.

```text
Beachhead A
→ Adjacent B is easier because [asset / distribution / credibility]
→ Adjacent C is easier because [new compounding advantage]
→ Economic power increases because [mechanism]
```

Ask:

- Which wedge creates credibility for the next?
- Which generates proprietary learning?
- Which creates integrations, installed base, data, community, or standards influence?
- Which can fund expansion?
- Does an early wedge trap positioning in a low-value category?
- Does each adjacency strengthen the original advantage or push the company toward commodity competition?

## Use scoring correctly

A scorecard is for exposing trade-offs.

Do not let a weighted average hide fatal flaws. A wedge with a high total score but no buyer, no distribution path, weak demand, or extreme platform risk may still be unacceptable.

Mark **gating conditions** separately.

Example:

```text
Wedge A score: 4.2 / 5
Gating issue: requires regulator approval that is unlikely within 24 months.
Verdict: not first wedge despite high theoretical attractiveness.
```

## Wedge recommendation

End with:

1. recommended wedge / beachhead
2. why now
3. customer and buyer
4. magnitude of customer advantage
5. time-to-value
6. distribution path
7. why it can be won deeply
8. what it accumulates
9. expansion geometry
10. expected economic-power mechanism
11. biggest risk
12. evidence that would cause selection of a different wedge
