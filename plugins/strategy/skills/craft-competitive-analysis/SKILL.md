# Competitive analysis guide

Competitive analysis answers one question: what could prevent this strategy from winning, now and after competitors react? A feature matrix is evidence, not the analysis.

Use the [competitive analysis](../business-strategy/assets/competitive-analysis.md) record as a starting shape. The method lives in [competitive dynamics](../business-strategy/references/competitive-dynamics.md), with [market entry](../business-strategy/references/market-entry.md) for competitor profiles and [market system mapping](../business-strategy/references/market-system-mapping.md) for capability and profit-pool structure. Run it inside [run-market-scan](../run-market-scan/SKILL.md), [define-positioning](../define-positioning/SKILL.md) or [set-strategy](../set-strategy/SKILL.md); it does not start a separate engagement.

## Choose the set from the decision

Profile only competitors that can change the decision. Build the set across:

- **product competitors** selling a closely substitutable outcome;
- **capability owners** whose assets the proposed solution depends on;
- **whole-vision competitors** that could combine existing assets and make the company unnecessary;
- the **status quo** — doing nothing, manual work, spreadsheets, internal build, consultants, open source and bundled "good enough" capability.

A list of every vendor in the category is a directory, not analysis. Say why each profiled competitor is in the set and which decision it informs.

## Profile for mechanism, not scorecards

For each competitor, establish:

```text
Segment and job served:
Why customers choose them today:
What they do materially better than we would:
Where their model constrains them:
  customer they must protect
  channel or partner they cannot bypass
  pricing or cost structure they cannot match
  architecture or product they cannot change quickly
  incentive that makes this market unattractive
Likely response to our move:
What would make the response faster or harder:
Evidence and date:
```

State plainly what they do better. An entry plan that assumes the incumbent is bad at the job is usually wrong about the customer. Then name the structural opening: unserved, underserved, structurally abandoned or temporarily ignored. A missing feature is not an opening unless the reason it is missing is durable and the buyer still has the problem.

## Project trajectory and bundle risk

Ask what the competitor can plausibly offer in 12–36 months given the assets it already owns — launches, acquisitions, hiring, platform direction, standards work, partnerships and what protects its core franchise. Distinguish capability from incentive: a platform can build something and still choose not to.

For bundle risk, assess the incremental cost to the buyer of the bundled option, procurement convenience, data and integration advantage, switching friction, the incumbent's incentive to bundle, and whether an independent vendor can remain materially better on a dimension the buyer values.

## Model response, then test the wedge

Run at least: no strong response, incumbent copies the core feature, incumbent bundles it, an adjacent platform enters, and price compression or open-source commoditization. For each, ask whether the wedge, positioning and economics still work. A competitive analysis that does not change a choice is a landscape description.

## Evidence discipline

Prefer primary sources: pricing pages, documentation, filings, release notes, job posts and direct product use. Cite the date, because competitor facts decay. Describe vendor claims as claims. Absence from a public page is not proof of absence; a public example does not settle licensing or roadmap. Search for disconfirming evidence and separate fact, reported estimate and inference.

## Review questions

1. Does each profiled competitor map to a decision, or is this a category list?
2. What does the strongest competitor do better, stated without hedging?
3. Is the opening structural and durable, or merely a gap someone has not shipped yet?
4. What response would make the wedge uneconomic, and how likely is it?
5. Does the analysis end in a changed choice for wedge, positioning or durability?

## Common failures

- Feature-matrix-only comparison.
- Measuring competitors by their weaknesses rather than their incentives.
- Treating "no direct competitor" as evidence of opportunity.
- Ignoring internal build, open source and bundled capability.
- Extrapolating every launch into inevitable dominance.
- Undated facts presented as current.
- A landscape that never states the strategic implication.
