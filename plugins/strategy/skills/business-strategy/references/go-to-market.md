# Go-to-Market

## Purpose

Go-to-market is how the chosen customer is reached, convinced and served at a cost the business can sustain. It is part of strategy because the motion determines the price point, the product surface, the sales cycle and the economics.

Use this reference after the wedge and value proposition are decided. Use [market entry](market-entry.md) for which market to enter, [pricing and packaging](pricing-and-packaging.md) for the commercial offer, and [operating plan](operating-plan.md) for how the motion is resourced and reviewed.

## Choose the motion

| Motion | Fits when | Economics it implies |
|---|---|---|
| Self-serve product-led | Low price point, quick time-to-value, user can adopt without approval | Low CAC, high volume, activation and retention do most of the work |
| Sales-led | High ACV, multiple stakeholders, procurement, implementation | Higher CAC, longer cycle, quota capacity constrains growth |
| Product-led sales | Users adopt first, buyer expansion follows | Hybrid; needs a trigger and a sales motion that does not damage the product experience |
| Partner or channel | The partner owns trust, access or implementation | Leverage, but dependency and diluted learning |
| Community or open-source-led | Technical buyers self-select and share knowledge | Adoption advantage, monetization separation, support burden |
| Services-assisted | The outcome requires configuration, migration or trust | Cash and learning early, product boundary unclear |
| Marketplace or embedded | The buyer already works inside a platform | Distribution and trust transfer, platform terms apply |

Selection questions:

- What does the buyer need to believe before purchase, and who can establish it?
- How long is the path from first contact to first value?
- Can the user adopt without the buyer, or must both move together?
- Which cost dominates: acquisition, implementation or support?
- What does the motion make the product become? A sales-led motion changes roadmap, pricing and support expectations.

Do not run two motions at once for the same segment unless they are separated by price, buyer or product surface. Mixed motions usually produce channel conflict and confusing signals to the customer.

## Channel scorecards

Evaluate channels with the same fields so comparisons are honest.

| Field | What to establish |
|---|---|
| Reach | Can it reach the beachhead at the required volume? |
| Intent | Does the audience have the problem now, or only an interest? |
| Cost | Fully loaded acquisition cost, including content, tools, commissions |
| Cycle | Time from first touch to revenue |
| Conversion | Qualified-to-close behavior, not raw leads |
| Control | Who owns the message, relationship and data |
| Learning | What the channel teaches about objections and use |
| Concentration | Dependency on one platform, partner or algorithm |
| Durability | Whether the channel remains available and affordable as the company grows |

Score qualitatively, then measure. A channel that looks cheap per lead but produces poor-fit customers is expensive.

Channel-specific checks:

- **Content and search** — intent match, competition, update dependence, time to rank.
- **Paid acquisition** — payback, creative fatigue, attribution, audience saturation.
- **Outbound** — list quality, deliverability, personalization cost, rep productivity.
- **Partners** — partner incentive, enablement cost, deal registration, conflict rules.
- **Marketplace** — listing visibility, fees, review dynamics, platform policy risk.
- **Community and open source** — contribution norms, support expectations, conversion path.
- **Events** — cost per qualified conversation, follow-up capacity.

## Launch sequence

A launch is a sequence of evidence gates, not a date.

```text
private design partners
→ limited availability with support
→ public availability with a repeatable motion
→ scaled acquisition
```

### Readiness gates

Before widening access, confirm:

- the entry offer delivers a complete outcome for the target job;
- onboarding reaches first value within a stated time;
- support load is understood and funded;
- billing, entitlements and terms work for the pricing model;
- security, privacy and legal claims are accurate;
- documentation matches the product;
- the team can handle the expected volume without breaking the experience.

### Launch types

Distinguish:

- **capability launch** — the product can now do something new;
- **market launch** — a new segment or geography is being served;
- **commercial launch** — price, packaging or terms change;
- **scale launch** — acquisition spend increases.

Each has different readiness criteria. A capability launch does not require a full commercial motion; a scale launch does.

## Growth loops

A loop compounds; a funnel spends. Identify which loop the motion relies on:

- **Viral** — use creates invitations or shared artifacts.
- **Content** — usage or expertise produces discoverable material.
- **Community** — participants help each other and create proof.
- **Integration** — presence in another product's workflow produces distribution.
- **Data or model** — usage improves the product in a way customers notice.
- **Sales learning** — each deal improves targeting and playbooks, reducing CAC over time.

For each loop, state:

```text
input → action → output → reinvestment → input
```

and the limiting factor: audience size, invitation rate, content production capacity, partner willingness, data rights, sales capacity.

A loop with no measured reinvestment is a diagram, not a growth mechanism. A loop that depends on a platform's algorithm is a rented channel; model what happens when terms change.

## Funnel metrics

Define each stage precisely; ambiguous definitions make comparisons meaningless.

| Stage | Example definition |
|---|---|
| Reach | Unique accounts exposed to the message in the period |
| Interest | Accounts that take a qualifying action |
| Qualified | Accounts matching the ICP with a live problem and reachable buyer |
| Activation | Accounts that reach the first meaningful outcome |
| Paid | Accounts with a completed purchase |
| Retained | Accounts still active at the cohort checkpoint |
| Expanded | Accounts increasing usage, seats or spend |

Guidance:

- Report cohorts, not only period totals.
- Separate new-logo from expansion revenue.
- Use account-level conversion for B2B, user-level for self-serve.
- State attribution method and its limits; multi-touch attribution often overstates measurable channels.
- Track cycle time and stage-to-stage conversion; a full funnel can hide a collapsing middle.
- Pair every lagging revenue metric with a leading behavior metric.
- Do not compare conversion rates across segments with different definitions.

## Beachhead and chasm

Crossing from early adopters to a mainstream segment is a distinct problem.

Early adopters buy on the promise of advantage and tolerate incompleteness. Mainstream buyers need:

- a complete solution for the job, including integrations and support;
- references from comparable organizations;
- proof of stability and security;
- a clear comparison with the incumbent;
- a reason the risk is acceptable.

Before moving to the mainstream segment, establish:

- reference customers in the target segment, not only enthusiasts;
- the "whole product" gaps and who fills them;
- the proof assets the buyer requires;
- the channel that reaches the mainstream buyer;
- the support and implementation capacity the segment expects.

Do not assume the early-adopter motion scales. A motion built on founder selling, design partners and manual onboarding usually breaks at the next segment.

## 90-day plan

A launch or motion change needs a bounded plan with evidence gates.

```text
Outcome (one sentence, observable):
Target segment and offer:
Motion and channels:
Capacity: people, budget, tools
Weeks 1–2: readiness and instrumentation
Weeks 3–6: first cohort and learning
Weeks 7–10: repeat and adjust
Weeks 11–13: decide scale, narrow or stop
Leading indicators and thresholds:
Owner and review cadence:
What would stop the plan:
```

Rules:

- One primary outcome. Multiple equal outcomes produce no prioritization.
- Instrument before launch; a launch without measurement is an anecdote.
- Include the support and follow-up capacity, not only acquisition.
- Define the decision at the end: scale, narrow, change motion or stop.
- Keep the plan consistent with the pricing and unit-economics assumptions.

## Failure modes

- Choosing a motion because competitors use it rather than because the buyer requires it.
- Launching broadly to learn what a narrow cohort would have taught cheaply.
- Counting signups as demand and activation as revenue.
- A channel scorecard with no measured cost.
- Growth loops drawn but never measured.
- Scaling acquisition before retention is understood.
- Running sales-led and self-serve motions against the same segment.
- Treating a launch date as a strategy milestone.
- Ignoring support load and implementation cost in the motion decision.
- Assuming early-adopter enthusiasm predicts mainstream adoption.

## Output

End with:

- the chosen motion and why it fits the buyer and economics;
- the channel scorecard with current evidence and gaps;
- the launch sequence and readiness gates;
- the growth loop and its limiting factor;
- funnel definitions, baselines and targets;
- the 90-day plan with decision points;
- the signals that would change the motion.

The useful result is a repeatable route to the customer, not a campaign calendar.
