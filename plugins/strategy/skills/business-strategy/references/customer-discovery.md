# Customer Discovery

## Purpose

Discovery replaces the team's model of the customer with observed reality. Its job is not to confirm the idea but to learn how the customer currently behaves, what the behavior costs, and what would have to change for a new choice to be made.

Use it for demand and switching questions. Use [customer behavior and culture](customer-behavior-and-culture.md) for the adoption mechanics that follow, and [market system mapping](market-system-mapping.md) when the buyer sits inside a larger value chain.

## Ideal customer profile

An ICP is a testable description of the customer for whom the proposed change is unusually compelling. It is not a demographic segment.

Define:

- **Role and circumstance** — who experiences the problem, in what situation, with what authority.
- **Trigger** — the event or threshold that makes the problem urgent now.
- **Job** — the progress the customer is trying to make, in their words.
- **Current alternative** — what they use or do today, including doing nothing.
- **Cost of the status quo** — money, time, risk, rework, missed revenue, political exposure.
- **Budget owner** — who pays, and from which budget.
- **Buying process** — who approves, blocks, evaluates and can veto.
- **Reachability** — where this group can be found and influenced at a cost the economics survive.

A useful ICP test:

> Could a salesperson or a landing page name this group, predict its objection and find it without buying a generic list?

If the answer is no, the segment is probably too broad to test.

Distinguish:

- the **user** who experiences the work;
- the **economic buyer** who releases budget;
- the **beneficiary** who gains when it works;
- the **blocker** who can stop adoption.

Products commonly fail because the pain is real for the user, the budget belongs elsewhere, and the blocker has no reason to accept the change. See [wedge selection](wedge-selection.md) for choosing the first segment and [value proposition](value-proposition.md) for making the offer relevant to each role.

## Map the status quo

Before asking what the customer wants, establish what happens today. The status quo is the real competitor.

Capture:

- the trigger and the sequence of work that follows;
- tools, spreadsheets, messages, approvals and handoffs in the current path;
- the workaround and its hidden cost — rework, delay, error rate, overtime, shadow tooling, consultant spend;
- who feels the cost, who causes it and who would have to change;
- what the customer has already tried and abandoned, and why;
- what they refuse to change;
- how success is judged and by whom.

Questions that produce usable detail:

- "Walk me through the last time this happened."
- "What did you do next?"
- "Who else was involved, and what did they need?"
- "What did that cost you — time, money, a delayed decision?"
- "What did you try before? Why did it stop?"

Ask about the last occurrence rather than a typical one. Specific past behavior is more reliable than a generalization.

## Behavioral interviews

### Structure

Use a short, consistent guide so evidence can be compared across participants. Keep it open enough that the customer's framing can appear.

1. **Context** — role, team, scope, the work they own.
2. **Recent instance** — the last time the problem occurred, reconstructed in sequence.
3. **Alternatives** — what they used, paid for, built or avoided.
4. **Consequence** — what it cost and who noticed.
5. **Change history** — what they adopted or abandoned, and why.
6. **Buying reality** — who approves spend, what the process requires.
7. **Closing** — who else experiences this, and what should be investigated next.

### Interview discipline

- **Ask about behavior, not hypotheticals.** "Would you use this?" produces politeness. "What did you do last time?" produces evidence.
- **Do not pitch.** Describing the solution converts the participant into a critic of your framing and ends discovery.
- **Do not lead.** Avoid embedding the answer: "How frustrating is the manual reconciliation?" assumes the emotion.
- **Follow the cost.** When the participant mentions a workaround, ask what it takes and who pays for it.
- **Separate roles.** Interview users and buyers separately; their accounts of the same process usually differ in informative ways.
- **Record the exact words.** Customer language is positioning material; paraphrase loses it.
- **Note what is absent.** A problem that never appears unprompted may not be salient.
- **Stop recruiting from one network.** Friends, former colleagues and existing users share your assumptions and your vocabulary.

### Sample logic

Use a small number of interviews to find patterns, then check whether the patterns repeat in a different route to the segment. State the selection method and its bias. Six conversations with people from one company or one community are one context, not a market.

Do not report percentages from a convenience sample. "Most of the people we spoke to" is honest; "72% of the market" is not.

## Jobs to be done

Frame discovery around the progress the customer is trying to make, not the product category.

For a specific circumstance, establish:

- the functional outcome the customer needs;
- the emotional and social stakes — confidence, blame, status, looking competent to peers;
- the alternative currently "hired" for the job;
- the **push** away from the current situation;
- the **pull** toward a new approach;
- the **anxiety** about changing;
- the **habit** that keeps the current behavior in place.

Push and anxiety usually decide adoption more than feature lists. A product that increases pull without reducing anxiety produces interest without switching.

Use jobs to refine the segment and the offer, not to produce a persona document. See the Jobs to Be Done guidance in [frameworks](frameworks.md) when the user asks for that lens explicitly.

## Commitment versus compliment

Discovery evidence sits on a ladder. Rank signals by what they cost the customer.

| Level | Signal | What it suggests | What it does not |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Interesting", "keep me posted" | Politeness or mild relevance | Any demand |
| 2 | Detailed problem description, unprompted | The problem is salient and articulable | That the customer will pay |
| 3 | Introduces a colleague or decision maker | Social capital is being spent | Budget or authority |
| 4 | Shares data, access or workflow artifacts | Real cooperation and trust | A purchase decision |
| 5 | Does manual work to try the outcome | Time commitment and perceived value | Repeatability |
| 6 | Agrees to a pilot with success criteria and a date | Operational commitment | That the criteria will be met |
| 7 | Pays, prepays or signs binding terms | Economic commitment | Retention, expansion or scale |
| 8 | Renews, expands or refers without prompting | Durable value and social proof | That the next segment behaves the same way |

Rules:

- Treat levels 1–2 as hypothesis generation, not validation.
- A compliment after a demo is the weakest possible signal; it measures social friction, not demand.
- Price the commitment appropriately. A symbolic deposit validates politeness; a real one validates budget.
- Ask for the next level explicitly and record the answer. "Who else should I speak to?" is cheap; "Can we run a two-week pilot with these three success measures?" is informative.
- Never upgrade a stated intention into a commitment in the notes.

## Discovery ledger

Keep one compact record. Reuse the evidence section of `strategy-state.md` when persistent state exists; do not maintain a separate research archive.

| ID | Participant (role, segment) | Observation or quote | Type | Strength | Implication | Next test |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| D01 | Operations lead, 40-person logistics team | Rebuilt last month's reconciliation in a spreadsheet after the tool missed a carrier change | behavior | moderate | The workflow has a recurring failure the buyer already pays to fix | Ask for the spreadsheet and the time it took |

Guidance:

- **Type** distinguishes what the participant did from what they said.
- **Strength** reflects directness, recency, independence and cost to the participant.
- **Implication** must be a decision-relevant statement, not a summary of the conversation.
- **Next test** names the cheapest way to raise or lower confidence.
- Group observations by circumstance and job, not by company size or job title alone.
- Keep disconfirming observations. A ledger that only supports the thesis is a marketing document.

## Evidence limits

State these limits whenever discovery informs a material decision:

- **Selection bias** — who was reachable, and who was excluded by the recruiting method.
- **Small samples** — patterns are hypotheses until they repeat in an independent route.
- **Leading questions** — the interviewer's framing shapes the answer.
- **Social desirability** — participants overstate good intentions and understate inertia.
- **Recall error** — reconstructed workflows are smoothed; artifacts are more reliable than memory.
- **Survivor bias** — only current users are visible; non-adopters and churned customers hold the strongest counter-evidence.
- **Insider knowledge** — the team's own experience may not generalize.
- **Language gap** — the customer's category words may not match the product's.
- **Temporal** — a workflow observed during a crisis may not represent steady state.

Do not convert discovery into market size, willingness to pay or retention claims. Those need their own evidence; see [market sizing](market-sizing.md), [pricing and packaging](pricing-and-packaging.md) and [experiments and PMF](experiments-and-pmf.md).

## Output

End with:

- the ICP and the roles inside it;
- the current workflow and its measured or estimated cost;
- the job, trigger and switching anxieties that matter;
- the strongest behavioral evidence and its limits;
- the assumptions that changed, with the ledger rows affected;
- the next cheapest evidence that would change the segment, offer or decision;
- an explicit statement of what remains unknown.

The useful result is a sharper decision about who to serve and what must change, not a longer interview summary.
