# Choice Cascade and Strategy Brief

## Purpose

Analysis becomes strategy only when it resolves into a small set of reinforcing choices that someone can act on and later test. This reference covers two complementary ways to structure those choices and the shape of the durable output.

Use it at the end of a substantial engagement and whenever a strategy must be stated for a decision maker. Use [strategy option set](strategy-option-set.md) to generate and compare alternatives, and [frameworks](frameworks.md) for the canonical logic of each named lens.

## Two structures

### Playing to Win choice cascade

A strategy is an integrated set of five choices:

1. **Winning aspiration** — what winning means, for whom, over what horizon.
2. **Where to play** — customers, segments, geographies, channels, categories, layers of the value chain.
3. **How to win** — the specific advantage that makes the company preferred in those arenas.
4. **Capabilities** — what the company must be distinctively good at.
5. **Management systems** — the measures, processes and governance that keep those capabilities and choices alive.

The cascade is a coherence test, not a form. Each choice must support the others.

Common incoherences:

- an aspiration to serve enterprises with a self-serve product and no support capacity;
- a where-to-play in a regulated market with no compliance capability;
- a how-to-win based on proprietary data with no data acquisition mechanism;
- capabilities that are listed but not funded;
- management systems that reward the old strategy.

### Rumelt kernel

For a strategy that must explain a challenge:

1. **Diagnosis** — the critical obstacle or structure, simplified without distortion.
2. **Guiding policy** — the overall approach to that obstacle.
3. **Coherent actions** — mutually reinforcing moves that implement the policy.

The kernel is useful when the strategy reads as a goal list. Goals are not a diagnosis; "grow 40%" says nothing about what stands in the way.

The two structures combine well:

```text
Rumelt diagnosis → guiding policy
Playing to Win: aspiration, where, how, capabilities, systems
```

Do not run both as separate documents that disagree. Use one as the spine and the other as a check.

## Three materially different options

Before choosing, hold at least three options that differ in customer, wedge, layer, channel or business model. See [strategy option set](strategy-option-set.md) for the comparison fields and the material-difference test.

For the brief, each option needs:

- the thesis in one paragraph;
- the customer and economic buyer;
- where it plays and how it wins;
- the economics that must hold;
- the capability it requires;
- the main risk and the evidence that would stop it.

Three variants of the same model are one option and two distractions.

## Exclusions

A strategy that does not exclude is a list of intentions.

State:

- customers and segments not served, for now or at all;
- jobs and use cases not addressed;
- channels not used;
- geographies not entered;
- product layers not owned;
- monetization models rejected;
- capabilities deliberately not built.

For each exclusion, give the reason:

- it dilutes the advantage;
- it requires a capability the company cannot fund;
- it invites a competitor the company cannot beat;
- it conflicts with the pricing or channel;
- it is a later option that would be damaged by doing it now.

Exclusions protect focus, but they must be revisited when the premise changes. An exclusion based on a false assumption becomes a self-imposed constraint.

## Falsifiers and opportunity cost

### Falsifiers

For each critical choice, state what observable result would show it is wrong:

```text
Choice: serve mid-market operations teams through a partner channel
Falsifier: partner-sourced deals convert below X% or renew below Y% while direct deals do not
Window: two quarters after the first ten partner-sourced deals
```

A falsifier needs an indicator, a threshold, a window and a decision it reopens. "The market changes" is not a falsifier.

### Opportunity cost

Every choice spends something that could have been used elsewhere:

- capital and runway;
- management attention;
- engineering capacity;
- a channel relationship;
- an option to enter another market later;
- brand and positioning room;
- strategic flexibility.

Name the best alternative that is being given up and why the trade is acceptable. A recommendation without opportunity cost is a preference.

## Strategy brief shape

The brief is the durable statement of the accepted or proposed strategy. Keep it short enough to be read in one sitting and specific enough to guide product and commercial decisions.

```text
1. Decision and status
   What is being decided, by whom, by when; proposed or accepted.

2. Situation and diagnosis
   The critical challenge, the evidence behind it, and why now.

3. Winning aspiration
   What winning means, for which customer, over what horizon.

4. Where to play
   Segment, buyer, geography, channel, layer; the beachhead and the destination.

5. How to win
   The value proposition, the advantage, the proof, and the main alternative.

6. Capabilities and management systems
   What must be distinctively good; how it is measured, funded and governed.

7. Economics
   Pricing and monetization, market size, unit economics, capital needs.

8. Exclusions
   What the strategy will not do, and why.

9. Options and opportunity cost
   The three options considered, why the others lost, what this choice forecloses.

10. Assumptions and falsifiers
    The critical assumptions, their confidence, the tests and the tripwires.

11. Next actions and review
    The next commitments, owners, dates and the review cadence.
```

Adapt the sections to the decision. A light engagement may need only decision, diagnosis, choices, economics, exclusions and tests.

### What the brief is not

- It is not a product requirements document. Requirements describe behavior for a specific product increment and belong to product management and the development lifecycle.
- It is not a roadmap. A roadmap sequences product outcomes and delivery over time and is owned by product direction.
- It is not an engineering change record. The change record tracks a specific change, its design, implementation and verification.
- It is not a pitch deck. The deck is a projection of the brief for a specific audience and must not become the authority.

The brief owns the business choices. Other artifacts own their own concerns and must remain consistent with it.

## Coherence checks

Before publishing the brief, test:

- **Cascade** — does each choice support the aspiration and the where-to-play?
- **Capability reality** — can the required capabilities be built or bought within the horizon, and are they funded?
- **System fit** — do incentives, metrics and governance reward the new strategy?
- **Economics** — does the pricing and cost structure survive the chosen segment and motion?
- **Competition** — does the advantage survive the most likely response?
- **Evidence** — is each critical claim graded, and are assumptions visible?
- **Exclusions** — is anything important left undecided because no one wanted to exclude it?
- **Testability** — can a reader tell what would change the strategy?

## Failure modes

- A cascade where "how to win" is a restatement of the product.
- An aspiration with no customer, horizon or definition of winning.
- Where-to-play lists that include every segment.
- Capabilities named but not resourced.
- Management systems copied from the previous strategy.
- A brief that hides disagreement behind neutral language.
- Multiple options described but only one analysed.
- Exclusions omitted to avoid conflict.
- Falsifiers that can never fire.
- A strategy brief that quietly becomes a PRD, roadmap or status report.

## Output

End with:

- the chosen strategy in the five choices, or the kernel;
- the three options and the reason each lost;
- the exclusions and opportunity cost;
- the economics and the evidence grade behind them;
- the critical assumptions, falsifiers and next tests;
- the review trigger and the status of the decision.

The useful result is a strategy a competent operator could execute and a skeptic could challenge.
