# Value Proposition

## Purpose

A value proposition states why a specific customer should change behavior in favor of this offer rather than the alternative they use today. It is a claim about cause and consequence, and it needs proof. The sequence below follows April Dunford's positioning approach; adapt it to the evidence rather than filling every step.

Use this reference when the product, positioning or sales message is being chosen or repaired. Use [positioning and category](positioning-and-category.md) for the category frame and comparison set, and [wedge selection](wedge-selection.md) when the question is which customer to serve first.

## Build it in order

A value proposition is assembled, not written.

```text
competitive alternative
→ unique attributes
→ customer value
→ proof
→ best-fit customer
→ category frame
→ switching anxieties
→ behavior test
```

Skipping to the sentence produces a slogan that cannot be defended in a sales conversation.

## 1. Competitive alternatives

Start with the real alternative, not the ideal one.

> If this offer did not exist, what would the customer use or do instead?

Include:

- doing nothing and tolerating the cost;
- manual work, spreadsheets and internal tools;
- internal build;
- an adjacent product that solves part of the job;
- a consultant or service provider;
- a platform's bundled "good enough" capability;
- an open-source component plus internal effort.

The status quo is usually the strongest competitor, because it requires no change. Compare against the alternative's actual performance and cost, not its marketing.

## 2. Unique attributes

List what this offer can do that the relevant alternatives cannot do well. Separate:

- **unique** — not available elsewhere at acceptable cost;
- **better** — available elsewhere, but materially improved;
- **table stakes** — required to be considered, not a reason to choose;
- **claims** — asserted without evidence.

Reject words that cannot be tested: innovative, easy, modern, AI-powered, enterprise-grade. Replace them with a mechanism:

```text
bad:  "AI-powered workflow"
good: "reads the existing ticket history and drafts the customer reply in the queue's own format"
```

If an attribute is not unique or better, it belongs in the proof of competence, not in the value proposition.

## 3. Customer value

Translate the attribute into a consequence for the customer:

```text
capability → operational effect → economic or strategic value
```

Ask:

- What becomes possible that was not possible before?
- What disappears: steps, delay, error, risk, headcount, vendor, approval?
- What improves: speed, quality, coverage, compliance, margin, decision confidence?
- Who captures that value — the user, the buyer, another function, or the customer's customer?
- Can it be expressed in the customer's own unit: hours, euros, incidents, cycle time, conversion, audit findings?

Classify the magnitude using the scale in [positioning and category](positioning-and-category.md): incremental, meaningfully better, dramatically better, or qualitatively new. The question is not whether the product is different but whether the difference is large enough to change purchasing behavior or the comparison set. A "10×" is a useful heuristic, not a requirement.

Value that accrues to the user while the cost falls on a different function is a budget problem, not a value problem. Name the payer and the beneficiary separately.

## 4. Proof

Every material claim needs a proof route appropriate to its type.

| Claim | Credible proof |
|---|---|
| Capability | Demonstrable product behavior in the customer's workflow |
| Performance | Measured result with method, baseline and conditions |
| Reliability | Observed behavior over time, incident history, guarantees |
| Economic value | Customer-side calculation with their inputs, or a paid outcome |
| Adoption | Reference customers with comparable circumstance |
| Trust | Security review, certification, audit, contractual commitments |
| Switching cost | Migration plan with time, effort and reversibility stated |

Rules:

- A demo proves the capability exists, not that the value materializes.
- A customer quote proves perception, not magnitude.
- An internal benchmark is not an independent result.
- Do not present a vendor claim as verified behavior; say who claims it and what was checked.
- Where proof does not exist yet, label the claim as a hypothesis and name the test that would produce proof.

## 5. Best-fit customer

The same proposition is stronger for some customers than others. Define the segment where the value is unusually acute:

- circumstance and trigger;
- the job and its frequency;
- the current alternative and its failure;
- the cost of the status quo in their terms;
- buying criteria and approval path;
- why this segment cares more than the average customer.

Prefer a segment where the advantage is obvious to a customer with a live problem over a broad segment where the product is somewhat better.

The best-fit customer is also a distribution decision: if the segment cannot be reached economically, the proposition is academically correct and commercially weak. See [customer discovery](customer-discovery.md) and [wedge selection](wedge-selection.md).

## 6. Jobs, pains and gains

Summarize the customer's world in their language, grounded in observed evidence:

- **Jobs** — the progress they are trying to make, functional and organizational.
- **Pains** — what blocks, delays, costs or embarrasses them today.
- **Gains** — the outcome they expect, and how they would recognize it.

Rank by frequency, severity, urgency and economic consequence. A frequent annoyance is not automatically a commercial problem; a rare but expensive failure often is.

Do not invent personas or quotes. If a job or pain has no observation behind it, mark it as an assumption. See [customer behavior and culture](customer-behavior-and-culture.md) for the emotional, social and organizational dimensions.

## 7. Category frame

The category tells the buyer what to compare the offer with and which budget to use. The options and their trade-offs are covered in [positioning and category](positioning-and-category.md); choose among them on the cost of education and the risk of inviting the wrong comparison.

Ask:

- Does this frame invite commodity price comparison?
- Does it attach the product to a budget that reflects the value?
- Can a competitor credibly claim equivalence inside the same frame?
- Does the frame survive the first sales conversation with a skeptical buyer?

## 8. Switching anxieties

Adoption fails when the value is clear and the change still feels unsafe. Name the anxieties and answer them with evidence:

| Anxiety | Form it takes | Counter-evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Status and blame | "If this fails, it is on me" | Reference customers in the same role, clear accountability model |
| Migration | "Moving our data will take months" | Migration plan, tested import, reversible cutover |
| Workflow | "My team will not change how they work" | Observed behavior in a pilot, training and fallback |
| Integration | "It will not fit our stack" | Working integration, named limitations |
| Procurement | "Adding a vendor is harder than the problem" | Existing contract path, security pack, pricing within authority |
| Political | "This makes another team look bad" | Stakeholder map, shared credit, governance |
| Identity | "This is not how we do things here" | Peer proof, gradual adoption path |
| Risk | "What if it is wrong or unavailable?" | Reversibility, guarantees, failure behavior |

Anxieties are not objections to overcome with argument. They are adoption costs to reduce with design, proof and commercial terms. Some customers should be lost because their anxiety is rational and the offer cannot yet answer it.

## 9. Test the proposition

A value proposition is validated by behavior change, not by comprehension.

Useful tests:

- win/loss interviews against the actual alternative;
- switching evidence — a customer moved from the old workflow, not merely added the product;
- willingness to pay above the alternative's cost;
- sales-cycle length and objection pattern by segment;
- activation and repeat use when self-serve;
- referral language — how customers describe the value unprompted.

If customers understand the message but do not switch, the problem is usually value magnitude, trust or change cost, not copy.

## Output

Write the proposition as a compact argument, not marketing copy:

```text
For [best-fit customer] in [circumstance],
the current alternative is [alternative] and it costs them [cost].
We [unique mechanism], which produces [operational effect]
and [economic/strategic value].
Proof: [strongest evidence and its limits].
Unlike [alternative], we [difference that changes the comparison].
This is not for [explicit exclusion].
Main switching anxiety: [anxiety] — answered by [evidence].
What would falsify this: [behavior that would not occur].
```

Then state:

- the magnitude of the advantage and the evidence behind it;
- the proof gaps and how they will be closed;
- the segment where the proposition is strongest;
- the exclusions that protect focus;
- the price and category implications.

## Failure modes

- Starting from the product's features instead of the customer's alternative.
- Claiming value without naming who captures it or who pays.
- Treating comprehension as demand.
- Writing a message for every segment, so it is decisive for none.
- Using a new category to avoid comparison rather than to explain value.
- Ignoring the status quo's switching cost.
- Copying competitor messaging without checking whether their customer is yours.
- Presenting a hypothesis as proof because it is well written.
