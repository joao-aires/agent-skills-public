# Named Strategy Frameworks

## Purpose

Use this file when the user explicitly asks for a named framework, thinker, or school of strategy, or when one of these lenses is clearly useful to a material strategic question.

The native `business-strategy` skill remains the integration layer. A framework should sharpen a question, not replace evidence, economics, or the persistent strategy state.

## Invocation Rules

When applying a named framework:

1. state which framework is being used and what question it is meant to answer;
2. apply its actual structure, not a generic strategy analysis with the framework name attached;
3. use current evidence for factual claims;
4. distinguish framework-derived interpretation from sourced fact;
5. identify the strategic implication, not only the completed canvas/matrix;
6. map material findings back to the six fundamental strategy questions;
7. update `strategy-state.md` and `decision-log.md` if the framework changes a material belief or choice.

If the user requests a framework not covered here, verify its canonical method before using it.

# Framework Guidance

## Jobs to Be Done (JTBD)

### Use when

- the problem definition is fuzzy;
- adoption/switching is more important than demographics;
- the team is describing features rather than customer progress;
- a new product may require behavior change.

### Apply

Focus on the **progress a customer is trying to make in a specific circumstance**.

Examine:

- triggering circumstance;
- desired functional outcome;
- relevant emotional/social dimensions;
- current workaround or alternative being "hired";
- push away from the current solution;
- pull toward the new solution;
- anxieties about switching;
- habits/inertia holding the customer back;
- hiring and firing criteria.

### Strategic implication

Use the result to refine problem definition, segment by circumstance/job where useful, identify true substitutes, and design wedge/positioning around the progress customers care about.

Do not reduce JTBD to a generic persona template.

## Playing to Win

### Use when

- the company needs a coherent strategy rather than an opportunity list;
- where-to-play and how-to-win choices are unclear;
- capabilities and operating systems must reinforce the chosen position.

### Apply

Work through the choice cascade:

1. **Winning aspiration** — what does winning mean?
2. **Where to play** — customers, geographies, channels, categories, stages of the value chain.
3. **How to win** — the specific advantage in those arenas.
4. **Capabilities** — what must the company be distinctively good at?
5. **Management systems** — what systems/metrics/processes sustain those capabilities and choices?

Treat the choices as mutually reinforcing, not independent checklist items.

### Strategic implication

Map the cascade into the current strategy thesis, especially where-to-play, how-to-win, required capabilities, exclusions, and implementation constraints.

## Porter Five Forces

### Use when

- the key question is industry attractiveness or profit-pool structure;
- pricing power or margin capture is uncertain;
- the company is entering an established market.

### Apply

Assess:

1. rivalry among existing competitors;
2. threat of new entrants;
3. supplier bargaining power;
4. buyer bargaining power;
5. threat of substitutes.

Focus on the **structural drivers** behind each force and how they affect prices, costs, investment requirements, and value capture.

### Strategic implication

Identify where industry structure makes profit capture easier or harder and whether the company can choose a position that changes exposure to the forces.

Do not turn Five Forces into a competitor feature table.

---

## Porter Strategic Positioning

### Use when

- differentiation is vague;
- the company is competing to be "better" rather than different;
- imitation risk is high.

### Apply

Define:

- a unique and valuable position;
- the distinct set of activities required to deliver it;
- trade-offs: what the company will deliberately not do;
- fit: how activities reinforce one another;
- whether the position supports premium price, lower delivered cost, or another durable economic advantage.

### Strategic implication

Translate differentiation from features into an activity system and explicit strategic exclusions.

---

## Zero to One / Peter Thiel

### Use when

- exploring novel businesses or new categories;
- the market is crowded and the user wants to escape commodity competition;
- a narrow beachhead may create disproportionate power;
- long-term value capture is central.

### Apply

Test:

- **secret / non-consensus insight** — what important truth may the market not yet recognize?
- **0→1 versus 1→n** — is this creating a new capability/market or copying an existing model?
- **magnitude** — is the product dramatically better or qualitatively new on an important dimension? Treat "10×" as a heuristic, not a literal rule.
- **beachhead dominance** — can a real, coherent small market be won deeply before expanding?
- **power characteristics** — proprietary advantage, network effects, economies of scale, brand/trust, or other mechanisms relevant to the case;
- **last-mover durability** — why can the company remain valuable after competitors and markets mature?

### Strategic implication

Use the lens to search for economic power and non-commodity positions, not to seek literal monopoly for its own sake.

Important safeguards:

- "no competition" can mean "no demand";
- do not manufacture a tiny category merely to claim monopoly;
- differentiate creative value capture from exclusionary/rent-seeking conduct;
- verify the non-consensus belief with evidence.

## Blue Ocean Strategy

### Use when

- head-to-head competition is unattractive;
- the user wants to redefine the value curve or create new demand;
- category boundaries may be changeable.

### Apply

Use:

- a **strategy canvas** to compare the factors the industry competes on;
- the **Four Actions Framework**:
  - Eliminate
  - Reduce
  - Raise
  - Create
- value innovation: seek a configuration that increases buyer value while changing the cost/value structure;
- examine noncustomers and barriers preventing adoption.

### Strategic implication

Produce a different basis of competition, then validate that the newly created value is wanted and economically capturable.

Do not treat an empty category as evidence of demand.

## Wardley Mapping

### Use when

- the business depends on a technology/value chain whose components are evolving at different rates;
- build/buy/partner decisions matter;
- commoditization and control points are strategic;
- today's differentiated capability may become utility infrastructure.

### Apply

1. anchor the map in a user need;
2. map the components/value chain required to serve it;
3. place components by evolution stage, typically from genesis/custom-built through product/commodity to utility;
4. reason about movement over time;
5. identify dependencies, inertia, opportunities, and which components should be differentiated versus consumed as commodities.

### Strategic implication

Use the map to avoid building strategy around a component likely to commoditize and to identify higher-order control, integration, or user-value layers.

Treat the map as a model for discussion, not a precise forecast.

---

## Crossing the Chasm

### Use when

- selling a discontinuous technology into B2B/mainstream markets;
- early enthusiast adoption is not converting into mainstream customers;
- a focused beachhead and references are strategically important.

### Apply

Distinguish early enthusiasts/visionaries from the pragmatic early majority. Define:

- a specific beachhead segment with a compelling reason to buy;
- the complete/"whole" product needed for that segment;
- reference customers and proof required for mainstream trust;
- positioning against the relevant alternative;
- adjacent segments to enter after establishing credibility.

### Strategic implication

Use it to sharpen wedge, product completeness, references, distribution, and expansion sequencing.

Do not assume every market follows the same adoption curve.

---

## 7 Powers

### Use when

- the business has product-market fit or a plausible winning position and the question is whether returns can persist;
- comparing alternative sources of durable economic power.

### Apply

Test the seven powers:

1. **Scale Economies**
2. **Network Economies**
3. **Counter-Positioning**
4. **Switching Costs**
5. **Branding**
6. **Cornered Resource**
7. **Process Power**

For each plausible power, identify both:

- the economic benefit to the company; and
- the barrier that prevents competitors from simply arbitraging it away.

### Strategic implication

Separate attractive product features from mechanisms capable of sustaining differential returns.

Do not assume every great business needs all seven powers.

---

## Rumelt Strategy Kernel

### Use when

- the strategy is vague, slogan-heavy, or a list of goals;
- the central challenge has not been diagnosed;
- actions do not appear coherent.

### Apply

1. **Diagnosis** — simplify reality into the critical challenge or structure that matters.
2. **Guiding policy** — define the overall approach for dealing with that challenge.
3. **Coherent actions** — specify mutually reinforcing actions that implement the policy.

### Strategic implication

Use the kernel to force prioritization and coherence. Goals without diagnosis and guiding policy are not strategy.

---

## Scenario Planning

### Use when

- structural uncertainty is high;
- a single forecast would create false precision;
- standards, regulation, technology, behavior, or market structure may diverge.

### Apply

- identify the uncertainties that matter most to the decision;
- construct a small number of internally coherent plausible futures;
- test the strategy in each;
- identify no-regret moves, options, vulnerabilities, signals, and tripwires.

### Strategic implication

Prefer strategies robust across plausible futures or explicitly accept the risk of a scenario-dependent bet.

Scenario planning is not prediction theater.

---

## Pre-mortem

### Use when

- red-teaming an apparently attractive strategy;
- optimism or confirmation bias may be high;
- the next step is expensive or difficult to reverse.

### Apply

Assume the strategy failed badly in the relevant future horizon and ask:

- what happened;
- which assumption was wrong;
- what customers/competitors/platforms did;
- what economic or distribution mechanism failed;
- what warning signs existed earlier.

Convert important failure modes into tests, mitigations, and tripwires.

---

## Ansoff Matrix

### Use when

The main question is growth direction.

### Apply

Explore four combinations:

- existing products × existing markets — market penetration;
- existing products × new markets — market development;
- new products × existing markets — product development;
- new products × new markets — diversification.

### Strategic implication

Use the matrix to broaden alternatives, then evaluate each option with the full strategy system. The matrix itself does not establish attractiveness or capability fit.

---

## Business Model Canvas

### Use when

- business-model design is underexplored;
- multiple monetization/delivery configurations are plausible;
- the user explicitly asks for the canvas.

### Apply

Cover:

- customer segments;
- value propositions;
- channels;
- customer relationships;
- revenue streams;
- key resources;
- key activities;
- key partnerships;
- cost structure.

### Strategic implication

Use it to generate and compare business-model configurations, especially how revenue model changes buyer, distribution, product scope, and cost structure.

Do not mistake a completed canvas for proof that the model works.

---

## SWOT

### Use when

The user explicitly wants a compact summary of internal strengths/weaknesses and external opportunities/threats.

### Apply

Keep entries specific and evidence-grounded. Then convert them into choices or tests.

### Strategic implication

SWOT is a synthesis aid, not a primary strategy engine. Avoid generic lists that do not change a decision.

# Combining Frameworks

Combine only when each framework resolves a different question. Useful pairings include:

- **JTBD + Porter positioning** — customer progress + differentiated activity system;
- **Playing to Win + 7 Powers** — integrated choices + durability;
- **Five Forces + 7 Powers** — industry economics + company-specific persistent advantage;
- **Zero to One + future-market scenarios** — non-consensus/power thesis + durability across futures;
- **Blue Ocean + JTBD** — altered value curve + evidence of real customer progress;
- **Wardley Mapping + future-market scenarios** — component evolution + alternative market structures;
- **Crossing the Chasm + wedge selection** — beachhead choice + GTM sequencing;
- **Scenario Planning + Pre-mortem** — external uncertainty + internal failure mechanisms.

Avoid running several frameworks merely to make an analysis appear comprehensive.
