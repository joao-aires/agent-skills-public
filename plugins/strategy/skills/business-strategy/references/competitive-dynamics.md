# Competitive Dynamics

## Principle

Competitive analysis should answer:

> What could prevent this strategy from winning, now and after competitors react?

A feature matrix is evidence, not the analysis.

## Three levels of competition

### 1. Product competitors

Who already sells the same or a closely substitutable outcome?

Capture:

- target customer
- positioning
- product scope
- pricing / business model
- distribution
- traction where knowable
- strengths
- weaknesses
- switching costs

### 2. Capability competitors

Who owns important pieces of the envisioned solution?

This matters especially for platforms and infrastructure. A company may have no direct competitor while every important capability is already owned by powerful adjacent vendors.

Map capabilities to owners and ask whether the proposed company is assembling commodity pieces or owning a genuinely scarce layer.

### 3. Whole-vision competitors

Which companies could combine assets they already control and make the proposed company unnecessary?

Examples of relevant assets:

- installed customer base
- identity / security
- cloud infrastructure
- operating system / platform
- distribution
- proprietary data
- ecosystem
- payment rails
- workflow ownership

The most dangerous future competitor may not currently market the same product.

## Competitor trajectory

For each strategically important competitor ask:

> What can this company plausibly offer in 12–36 months given the assets it already owns?

Look at:

- recent launches
- acquisitions
- hiring
- platform direction
- standards participation
- partnerships
- bundling incentives
- what protects its core franchise

Do not extrapolate every feature into inevitable dominance; distinguish capability from incentive.

## Bundle risk

A standalone product can lose even when it is better if an incumbent can bundle "good enough" functionality into an existing contract or workflow.

Assess:

- incremental buyer cost of the bundled option
- procurement convenience
- data/integration advantage
- switching friction
- incumbent incentive to bundle
- whether the independent vendor can remain 10× better on a valuable dimension

## Internal build and open source

For technical markets, internal build and OSS are often primary substitutes.

Ask:

- How hard is the minimal solution to build?
- Is operational burden recurring or mostly one-time?
- Does the customer value control more than convenience?
- Which capabilities become commodities through open source?
- What remains painful enough to pay for?

## Competitive response scenarios

For a chosen strategy, model at least:

- no strong response
- incumbent copies core feature
- incumbent bundles it
- adjacent platform enters
- price compression / open-source commoditization

Then ask whether the wedge still works.

## Positioning map

Use dimensions that customers actually care about, not generic axes chosen for a pretty chart.

Possible dimensions:

- control versus convenience
- point solution versus platform
- self-serve versus enterprise-managed
- closed versus open ecosystem
- low-cost versus high-assurance

## Output

End with:

- competitors that matter today
- capabilities that are becoming table stakes
- future entrants that matter most
- likely competitive reaction
- remaining whitespace
- strategic implication for wedge, differentiation, and control point

The conclusion should change the strategy, not merely document the landscape.
